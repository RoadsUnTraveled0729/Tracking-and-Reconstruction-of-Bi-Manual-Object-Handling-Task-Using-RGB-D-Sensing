  1  Chapter 5: Pose Recovery During Tracking Failure
  2  Chapter 3 solved the joint angles of the upper body from the eight filtered landmarks of Chapter 2, and every landmark of the chain has to be measured for that solve to run. Chapter 4 delivered an independent track of the carried object in the same metric scene, a measured cube pose or a marked gap on every frame. Neither chapter settles the frame on which the landmark detector reports no wrist, or a wrist whose depth belongs to the cube in front of it. The arm is then left without a state to solve from, while the object in that same hand is still measured. When body landmarks become unreliable, object observations provide an additional geometric constraint on where the arm can be, and this chapter is the layer that forms that constraint and uses it, between the filtering of Chapter 2 and the solve of Chapter 3.
  3  The chapter treats occlusion as missing data in an otherwise clean recording. The torso is measured on every frame, so the root frame of Chapter 3 is always available and the recovery works on the arms. Section 5.1 states these assumptions and the parameters.
  4  The arm chain of Chapter 3 runs from the shoulder through the elbow to the wrist, with the calibrated upper-arm length [L1] and forearm length [L2]. A missing landmark is rebuilt from the landmarks still measured and from state carried over from earlier frames, in three cases. In Case A the wrist is missing and the shoulder and elbow are measured: the wrist is placed at the forearm length from the elbow, along the remembered forearm direction (Section 5.3). In Case B the wrist is missing while that hand holds the cube: the wrist is placed from the measured object pose of Chapter 4 and the hand-object offset h (Section 5.4). Case B takes priority over Case A, because the object pose is measured on the same frame. In Case C the elbow is missing and the shoulder and wrist are known, measured or rebuilt: the elbow is placed by two-link inverse kinematics on the two calibrated lengths (Section 5.5).
  5  Where no case anchors a landmark, the joint that needs it holds its last angle, and the output says so. The solve of Chapter 3 produces seven joint groups, the root and three per arm, and each leaves the layer in one of three states. A group is measured when every landmark it needs was measured and unflagged on this frame, held when it keeps the value it last had, and constrained when it was solved from a rebuilt landmark or with its change limited. The states travel with the angles: Chapter 6 draws a held joint and a rebuilt joint in different colours, and Chapter 7 separates measured frames from reconstructed ones.

  9  5.1 Assumptions and Parameters
 10  The recovery rests on six assumptions about the recording and the subject, and Chapter 9 takes up what follows when they fail. The recording is mostly clean: every landmark is measured on most frames, a failure is a short window, and the recovery fills such windows without tracking a hidden arm for long. The torso is measured on every frame. The two hips and the two shoulders are taken as correct, landmark 24 above all, since it is the origin of the root frame of Chapter 3. A lost root frame is outside the scope of this thesis. The recording of this thesis needs one preparation for this to hold. The hip landmarks take the depth of the rail in front of the pelvis, so the pipeline replaces the hip depth with the value remembered from clean frames before the solve, the preparation of Section 2.6 outside this layer. The plain, direction-memory and object-assisted solves of Chapter 7 all rest on it, and the hold-last method, which keeps the last accepted joint angles, does not. The two torso detectors of Section 5.2 report the frames on which the assumption fails.
 11  The grasp is rigid within one grip episode: while a hand holds the cube the wrist keeps a fixed position in the mapped marker frame {MappedMarker} that Chapter 6 establishes, and a regrasp starts a new episode and a new offset. The object marker stays visible while the hand is hidden. Case B needs a measured object pose on the frame it repairs, and where the marker is lost as well, Case A or the held angle takes over. The bone lengths are constant, so the upper arm and the forearm keep their calibrated lengths through the recording. The subject moves slowly, as the task of Chapter 2 is defined, so a landmark cannot jump across the scene between two frames and the direction of a segment changes little from one frame to the next. The direction memories of Section 5.3 rely on this.
 12  The recovery needs parameters of three kinds, each stated where its rule is defined. The calibrated constants are the upper-arm and forearm lengths, taken from the clean frames at the start of the recording or from an earlier calibration. They also include the fixed mapping derived from the scene calibration, swap, gravity alignment and floor placement, which carries a point of {Scene} into {Camera'} (Section 5.3). The design values were fixed before the recording. They are the detector thresholds and the mask cleaning of Section 5.2, the gains, radii, entry and exit runs and memory horizon of Section 5.3, the offset bound of Section 5.4, and the reach guard, twist hold and rate limit of Section 5.5. The states are estimated on clean frames of the recording and carried forward: the hand-object offset per grip episode, the holding state of each hand, and the direction memories of the two arms. Flagged arm frames do not enter the local updates or episode means; Section 5.3 distinguishes the earlier recording-wide fallback fit.
 13  5.2 Landmark Tracking Failures
 14  A landmark can be wrong in two ways, and only one of them is announced. The landmark extraction of Section 2.3 attaches a source flag to every sample. That flag says whether the point was usable, whether its visibility score fell below the gate of that section, or whether the depth window around its pixel held no valid depth sample (Appendix D). A sample with either of the last two flags is a declared failure, already treated as missing. The second way is silent. The pixel is correct and the visibility score is high, but the depth reading behind that pixel belongs to a hand, to the carried cube, or to the desk in front of the subject. The point is then lifted tens of centimetres away from the body. The visibility score cannot show this, because it describes the image only.
 15  Geometry shows it. Over a few frames the body is rigid: a limb segment cannot change length, the trunk cannot fold, and a landmark cannot jump across the scene between two frames of a slow task. Each statement becomes a test per frame, and a frame that fails one carries a wrong measurement whatever the detector says. A further test uses the object: a hand that holds the cube cannot have its wrist far from it. Figure 5.2 draws the six tests.
 16  [FIGURE]
 17  Figure 5.2. The six failure detectors, one panel each. The dark skeleton is the correct geometry and the red element is the condition that fires the detector. The upper row shows the acquisition, segment-length and line-angle conditions, and the lower row the width-ratio, landmark-step and grip-plausibility conditions.
 18  The acquisition detector is a report rather than a test: it fires wherever the landmark detector marks a sample as low in visibility or without depth. The four geometry detectors turn the rigid-body statements into numbers, the trunk statement giving two of them. The segment-length detector compares the upper arm and forearm of an arm with the median of that segment over the clean frames of the recording, and fires at a departure of 35 percent. The line-angle detector measures the angle between the hip line and the shoulder line, which stay close to parallel while the trunk is one rigid plate, and fires above 22 degrees. The width-ratio detector compares the ratio of shoulder width to hip width with its clean median, fixed by the subject's build, and fires at a departure of 20 percent. The landmark-step detector measures how far each landmark moved between two consecutive usable frames, and fires above 5 centimetres.
 19  The grip-plausibility detector uses the object. While a hand holds the cube, the wrist and the object move together, so a wrist reported more than 35 centimetres from the object centre, the release radius of Section 5.3, is a wrong measurement and not a release. That bound is a design choice for the cube and grip of this task; its suitability for another object or recording has not been established. The four geometry thresholds are properties of a human body and of a slow task, and each is set at the 99th percentile of the clean frames of a separate reference recording plus a safety margin. That recording is clean for the right arm and the torso but not for the left arm, where for a stretch of frames the elbow slides along the limb while the upper arm and the forearm trade length, and the segment-length detector fires. Widening the tolerance until it stops firing would calibrate the threshold to a defect, so the threshold stays where the clean statistics put it.
 20  The per-frame results are combined into three failure masks, one per arm and one for the torso, each set whenever any of its detectors fires and then cleaned twice. Gaps of at most five frames are closed, so one frame on which the landmark detector recovers does not split a failure in two, and runs shorter than five frames are dropped, so one noisy frame does not open a failure window. The grip-plausibility test runs afterwards, because it needs the holding state of Section 5.3, which is built on the cleaned masks, and whatever it flags is added to the arm mask.
 21  A flagged arm has its elbow and wrist landmarks removed before the solve, so the detectors turn a wrong measurement into a missing one. This layer can rebuild or hold an absent landmark, while the solve of Chapter 3 has no defence against one that is present and wrong. The layer reports the torso mask and rebuilds no torso landmark, since under the assumption of Section 5.1 the torso is measured on every frame, after the hip depth preparation named there, and the two torso detectors only show where that assumption fails. What can be put back in place of the removed arm points starts from the object marker, which stays in clear view on the carried cube while the hand behind it is hidden.

 64  A single offset per recording will not do. The subject may let go of the cube and take it again, and each new grasp puts the hand somewhere else on the cube. Each grasp is one grip episode, the unit the rest of this chapter and Chapters 8 and 9 work in, and the offset belongs to the episode, not to the recording. Within one long episode the hand also shifts a little, so the offset is estimated with a short memory instead of fitted once. On each frame that contributes a clean observation, the estimate moves towards that observation by a small gain [c],
 65  [MATH] ​MappedMarkerhnew = 1 − c ​MappedMarkerhold + c ​MappedMarkerhk   (5.4)
 66  Equation (5.4) is a first-order recursive low-pass update, the cheapest smoother that can run on a stream of samples [55]. It mixes a little of the new observation into the previous estimate, so the estimate follows the current offset and forgets an old one, and the gain of 0.02 weights it towards about the last fifty frames. The estimator has three further rules. A frame that contributes no clean observation leaves the estimate untouched, so through a failure window the value in use is the offset from just before tracking was lost. The estimate restarts with each grip episode, because a new grasp is a new offset. Before five clean observations have initialized the local estimate, the mean of equation (5.3) over that episode stands in. This fallback uses the recording-wide fit if the episode has fewer than fifteen clean observations; once the local estimate is available, it takes priority. Flagged arm frames do not enter the local updates or episode means. The recording-wide fallback uses the earlier holding fit, which applies acquisition and forearm-plausibility checks rather than the later cleaned arm failure mask.
 67  The short memory has a limit of its own. The estimate follows a shift of the grip with a lag set by the gain, so the value frozen at a failure can still carry the offset of the last clean frames rather than the offset in force when tracking was lost. The grasp is not always as rigid as Section 5.1 assumes. On the loop recording the hand wraps around the cube and shifts on it. Over the frames on which the left hand holds the cube, the per-frame offset observations scatter with a standard deviation of 7.0, 2.7 and 6.3 centimetres on the three mapped marker axes. A non-rigid grip and the lag of the estimator are therefore two conditions under which the constraint the object supplies is itself wrong, and Section 7.4.2 reports what they cost on the natural failure windows.
 68  Equation (5.1) applies only to a hand that holds the object, so the layer must decide which hand that is. On a frame where everything is measured the decision takes five conditions together. The marker is measured, the object is away from its resting place, and the wrist is measured and unflagged. The wrist lies within the hold radius of the object centre, and the forearm of that arm measures within 30 percent of its own median length. The last condition rejects frames where the elbow and the wrist take their depth from the same near surface and collapse onto each other, which would otherwise put a plausible wrist next to the cube. The test needs a measured wrist, which is missing on the frames the recovery exists for, so the holding decision is carried as a state per hand, with rules for entering and leaving it.
 69  The hand enters the holding state after the instantaneous test passes on five consecutive frames. It stays in that state through a wrist failure for as long as the object is still being carried: a moving object that nobody was seen to release is still in the hand that held it. It leaves the state when the object comes to rest, or when a cleanly measured wrist sits farther than the release radius from the object centre, in either case over five consecutive frames. The release radius of 35 centimetres is wider than the hold radius of 25 centimetres, so the state does not flicker at the boundary. The run of frames between entry and exit is one grip episode. Both hands may hold at once, and a handover is two grip episodes that overlap. Figure 5.4 draws the state and its rules. In the offline preparation the state is set after the run that confirms it. Once the five-frame run confirms an entry, the state starts from the first frame of that run, and once an exit is confirmed the five frames of the exit run are cleared. The warm-up mean of an episode is likewise taken over the whole episode. Section 8.2 describes the version that decides with a delay instead.

 85  5.5 Elbow Recovery by Two-Link Inverse Kinematics
 86  Case C starts from a shoulder and a wrist, and the elbow follows from the arm's own dimensions. The upper arm and the forearm are two rigid links of known length, so placing the elbow is the two-link inverse kinematics problem of a planar manipulator lifted into three dimensions [42]. The two lengths are the calibrated [L1] and [L2] of Section 5.3. The construction below is written with the measured wrist [​Camera'pwr]. On a failure frame of a holding hand the object-derived estimate [​Camera'w] of equation (5.7) takes its place, once it has been carried into the y-up camera frame, and nothing else changes. The elbow is any point that satisfies both
 87  [MATH] ​Camera'pel − ​Camera'psh = L1,        ​Camera'pel − ​Camera'pwr = L2   (5.8)
 88  The first condition places the elbow on a sphere of radius [L1] about the shoulder, and the second on a sphere of radius [L2] about the wrist. The two spheres meet in a circle when their centres are closer than the sum of the radii and farther apart than the difference. That circle is the set of all elbows consistent with the two endpoints.
 89  Finding the circle starts from the triangle. Let [b] be the vector from the shoulder to the wrist, [r] its length, and [b] the unit vector along it. The triangle with vertices at the shoulder, the elbow and the wrist has sides [L1], [L2] and [r], all three known. The law of cosines then fixes its angle [j] at the shoulder, between the upper arm and the shoulder-to-wrist line:
 90  [MATH] cos j = L12 + r2 − L222 L1 r   (5.9)
 91  Dropping a perpendicular from the elbow onto the shoulder-to-wrist line completes the construction.  98  Equation (5.12) minimises, in closed form, the distance from [pel] to the point [psh + L1 u] over the circle, which is the elbow the remembered direction would give if it were reachable. Every point of the circle lies at the fixed distance [rc] from the centre, so the squared distance to that target changes with [s] only through the dot product of the circle vector with the displacement from the centre to the target. The part of that displacement along [b] contributes nothing, and the remaining part is [L1 u⊥], so the distance is smallest when the circle vector points along [u⊥]. The elbow therefore moves as little as possible from the position predicted by the remembered direction at the current shoulder. A downward direction then takes its place, since a relaxed elbow hangs, with the depth axis of the y-up camera frame as the third candidate; the two are perpendicular, so one of them always gives a point of the circle. 101  The construction is exact only while the shoulder-to-wrist distance lies between the difference and the sum of the two link lengths. Outside that range no elbow satisfies both conditions of equation (5.8), and the layer clips the cosine of equation (5.9) into its valid range instead. A wrist estimate beyond the reach of the two links clips the cosine at one, the circle radius of equation (5.10) falls to zero, and the elbow collapses onto the shoulder-to-wrist line as a straight arm pointing at the object. A wrist closer to the shoulder than the difference of the lengths pushes the cosine above one as well, since the upper arm is the longer link, and clips at the same end. In both cases the elbow keeps the upper-arm length and the forearm is left longer or shorter than its calibrated value, so the fallback approximates the elbow and satisfies only the upper-arm condition.
102  A single configuration has no answer: a wrist estimate that coincides with the shoulder leaves the shoulder-to-wrist line without a direction, on which the whole construction hangs, so the two-link solve returns nothing. The elbow is then placed from the shoulder along the upper-arm memory, and the groups that use it leave the layer constrained, not held. Only without a fresh memory does the arm fall back on holding its last angles.
103  Equation (5.9) is ill conditioned near a straight arm. Differentiating that equation, the sensitivity of [j] to [r] grows without bound as [r] approaches [L1 + L2]. Near full extension a small error in the wrist estimate becomes a large error in the flexion angle, because the triangle is nearly flat and its shape stops depending smoothly on its longest side. Above 98 percent of the full reach the elbow is therefore taken from the direction memory instead, with the wrist still anchored at the object-derived estimate, so on those frames the forearm is not in general at its calibrated length either. The two-link solution stays as the fallback where no fresh memory exists.
104  The layer applies two rules to the angles after the solve. The shoulder twist is read from the part of the forearm that is perpendicular to the upper arm, and that part vanishes as the elbow straightens, as Section 3.4.3 sets out. Below 15 degrees of elbow flexion a twist solved from measured landmarks keeps its previous value and its group leaves the layer held. The hold is released only above 25 degrees, so that the twist resumes on a measurement worth having. The hold does not reach a twist solved on a rebuilt arm: that group has already left the layer constrained, and below 15 degrees of flexion its twist is bounded only by the rate limit that follows. Every arm angle then passes a limit of 15 degrees of change between consecutive frames, which bounds the step the output takes when a joint re-locks after a hold or moves into a reconstruction. While the limit is active the group is marked constrained until the limited and the solved value agree again. The limit applies to the arm angles and not to the root.

132  The chapter has built the bridge between the two branches. The detectors of Section 5.2 say when a landmark cannot be trusted, and the offset of Section 5.3 says what the object can contribute. Cases A, B and C place a missing wrist or elbow from that contribution, from the remembered directions and from the calibrated lengths. The layer hands the solve of Chapter 3 an assembled set of landmarks on every frame, with each joint group tagged measured, held or constrained. Those tags are of no use until something carries them, which is the integration of Chapter 6. Whether the added constraint helps on a real failure depends on the object being visible and on everything Section 5.4 lists. Section 7.4 grades it, against the unmasked solve under synthetic masking and against the manual wrist labels, and Section 9.3 reads the two outcomes. Chapter 8 describes the version of the layer that runs while the motion happens.
